Research Spotlight: Clinical Case Writing for Beginners

A practical guide produced by the Research sector on how to structure and submit your first clinical case report — from bedside observation to publication.

MedicineBank Research Sector
8 min read · Updated April 2026
👁 4.2K reads 💬 12 comments

Case reports remain one of the most accessible entry points into published clinical research for undergraduate students.

Every clinician remembers the first patient whose presentation didn't quite fit the textbook. For most, that moment passes as an interesting anecdote shared over lunch. For a small number of medical students, it becomes something more durable: a published clinical case report, and often, a first line on a CV that would otherwise be empty of research experience.

The clinical case report is the most approachable form of medical literature to write, and also one of the most frequently done badly. This guide exists to close that gap — not by making the process sound simpler than it is, but by mapping the exact sequence a student can follow from the moment they notice something worth writing about to the moment a journal accepts it.

Section OneWhat actually makes a case "reportable"

Not every unusual patient is a publishable one. Editors are looking for a specific kind of novelty, and it rarely means "a disease I've never heard of." More often, reportable cases fall into one of a few recognizable categories: an atypical presentation of a common condition, an unexpected response to a standard treatment, a diagnostic pathway that took an unusual number of steps to resolve, or a rare adverse event tied to a medication or intervention.

The single most common rejection reason at this stage isn't poor writing — it's a case that is interesting to the student but not novel to the field. Before drafting a single sentence, a fast literature check answers the only question that matters early on: has this specific presentation already been described? If the answer is yes, the case may still be worth writing, but the angle has to shift toward what's genuinely new about this instance.

Before you start writing

Search PubMed and Google Scholar for the condition plus the unusual feature you noticed. If three or more similar reports already exist, your case needs a distinguishing angle — a different demographic, a different complication, or a different diagnostic route — to justify a new submission.

Section TwoSecuring consent and clearance early

This is the step students most often defer and most often regret deferring. Patient consent for case report publication is not a formality to collect once the manuscript is finished — it needs to happen as close as possible to the clinical encounter, while the patient (or their guardian) can be properly informed about what will be shared, even in de-identified form.

Institutional requirements vary, but the underlying principle doesn't: a case report describes a real person, and their right to decline publication has to be respected regardless of how academically valuable the case is. Most journals will not proceed to review without a signed consent form, and a supervising physician's sign-off is typically expected before any drafting begins in earnest.

Treat consent as the first deliverable of the project, not the last. A compelling case with no consent on file is not a draft — it's a dead end. — MedicineBank Research Sector, mentorship notes

Section ThreeStructuring the manuscript

Case reports follow a tighter, more predictable structure than most other article types, which is precisely what makes them approachable for a first-time author. Most journals expect some version of the following sequence:

  • Abstract — a compressed summary, typically under 250 words, stating the presentation, the key finding, and why it matters.
  • Introduction — brief context on the condition and precisely what makes this instance worth reporting.
  • Case presentation — the clinical narrative itself: history, examination findings, investigations, and management, told in chronological order.
  • Discussion — the section where the case is placed in conversation with existing literature, and where its novelty is argued rather than merely asserted.
  • Conclusion — a short, specific takeaway for the reader, not a restatement of the abstract.

The discussion section is where most first drafts fall short. It's tempting to simply retell the case a second time; the section instead needs to do argumentative work, actively drawing on the literature search from Section One to explain exactly what this case adds that prior reports did not.

Section FourChoosing where to submit

Case reports have a wide range of homes, from dedicated case report journals to case sections within general medical journals. For a first submission, dedicated case report journals are usually the more realistic target — review timelines tend to be shorter, and editorial expectations are calibrated specifically for this format rather than treating it as a lesser cousin of an original research article.

It's worth checking a target journal's specific case report guidelines before submission, since word counts, required checklists (such as CARE guidelines, which many journals now mandate), and figure requirements differ meaningfully between publications.

Section FiveWhat mentorship looks like in practice

Nearly every successful first-time case report has a supervising physician attached to it, and that relationship does more than satisfy an institutional requirement. A mentor catches the clinical nuance a student may not yet have the experience to weigh correctly, flags consent and ethical issues before they become submission blockers, and — often most usefully — tells a student honestly when a case isn't yet reportable, saving weeks of drafting a manuscript no journal will accept.

Students working through MedicineBank's Research sector are paired with a mentor at the literature-review stage specifically so that this feedback arrives before the writing begins, not after.
